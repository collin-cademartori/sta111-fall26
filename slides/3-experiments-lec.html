Data Collection 2: Observational Studies and Experiments

A Little Causality

Relationships Between Variables

The goal of data collection is often to establish relationships between measured variables

Statistical Association

Definition: Association

Two variables have a statistical association in a dataset if one variable can be used to predict the other variable. Two numeric variables are positively associated if they tend to increase/decrease together, and they are negatively associated if one variable tends to increase when the other decreases, and vice versa.

Note:

  1. Association is symmetric: If \(X\) is associated with \(Y\), then \(Y\) is associated with \(X\)
  2. Association can arise for many different reasons, and it is often hard to know why two variables are associated.

Causality

Definition: Causal Relation

A variable \(X\) has a causal effect on variable \(Y\) if changing the value of \(X\) would also change the value of \(Y\) (all else equal).

Notes:

  1. Causality is hard to establish because it requires knowing what value \(Y\) would take if \(X\) took its original value and if \(X\) took some new value.
  2. Unlike mere statistical association, a causal effect is not symmetric: If \(X\) has a causal effect, \(Y\) typically does not have a causal effect on \(X\).
  3. A common source of error is assuming causal effects from statistical associations.

Justifying Causal Claims

You Can’t Claim Causality Casually

Causality is often impossible to establish from data that is not collected carefully.

Definition: Observational Study

When researchers collect data from a process that they don’t control or manipulate, they are performing an observational study.

Examples:

  1. Political polls and other surveys
  2. Cross-sectional study (measuring a sample from a population at a fixed time)
  3. Cohort studies (following a group of individuals over time)

Weaknesses and Strengths of Observational Studies

  1. Observational studies rarely justify causal conclusions. Without further information, you may assume causal conclusions are never justified from an observational study.
  2. They are usually less expensive than other types of studies, and can be used to form hypotheses that can be tested later.
  3. While we typically can only infer statistical association from observational studies, it is sometimes easier to generalize those assoications to populations of interest because we did not interfere with the process producing the data.

Testing Causal Hypotheses

A causal hypothesis states that an explanatory variable causally effects a response variable.

A causal hypothesis must be specific to be testable. It must tell us under what conditions and over what time horizon the effect occurs, and ideally also specifies an anticiapted size for the effect.

Example: Taking a particular medication causes blood pressure to fall by at least 5% on average after a month among people with stage 2 hypertension.

A Note About Averages

Notice in the last example we said:

…causes blood pressure to fall by at least 5% on average…

This is easier to demonstrate than if we said in each patient.

Dicussion Question

Try to explain why it is easier to establish evidence for a causal claim about averages than about individual cases?

  1. To know what would happen to you, we’d have to observe you under both conditions (e.g. taking versus not taking the medication), which is impossible to do at the same time.
  2. To establish the average claim, it is enough to compare to groups which are alike in every way except the explanatory variable

Randomized Experiments

Definition: Randomized Experiment

To investigate a causal hypothesis, researchers may conduct a randomized experiment in which participants are randomly assigned to groups in which the researchers assign or control the value of the explanatory variable. Often times, the explanatory variable is the presence or absence of a treatment (such as a medication), in which case the group that receives the treatment is the treatment group, and the group that does not receive it is the control group.

Tip

What is the difference between a random sample and a randomized experiment? Can these two different forms of randomness be combined?

Randomization and Causality

  1. By randomizing individuals between groups, the researchers aim to produce groups that are alike in all variables except the explanatory and response variables.
  2. If they succeed, then any difference observed in the response variable can only be explained by the difference in the explanatory variable, justifying causal conclusions.
  3. Randomization can fail to produce similar treatment and control groups if the number of experiment participants is too small

Placebos, Blinding, and Controlling

  1. Members of the control group are often given a placebo: something indistinguishable from the treatment but which is known to have no effect (e.g. a sugar pill).
  2. Placebos are important if knowledge of your group can effect your response variable.
  3. When participants don’t know what group they’re in, the study in blind. When the researchers also don’t know the treatment assignments, the study is double-blind.
  4. In general, researchers try to control any additional variables that might introduce differences between the treatment and control groups, attempting to assure any such variables are equal in the two groups throughout the study.
  5. Randomization, placebos, and blinding are all ways of controlling differences between treatment and control groups.

Limitations of Experiments

Sometimes randomized are only possible with biased, unrepresentative samples

Example: Pregnant women are excluded from the majority of clinical trials in order to eliminate the possibility of serious side effects in their unborn children

Consequently, less is known about whether and how many drugs affect pregnant women and their childen

The Project STAR Experiment

Between 1985 and 1989, Tennessee randomized a cohort of teachers and students between kindergarten and third grade across 79 to small or large classrooms, finding strong positive effects of small classrooms on students learning.
